While I agree strongly with Li's analysis of the problem, however, I disagree with his solution, which is to use the central government balance sheet to clean up local government balance sheets so that they can return to their old mission of pumping up economic activity. "The crux of the problem," he says, "is the blockage, and the blockage lies in local-government debt. The answer is therefore obvious: the central government, and indeed the public sector as a whole, should expand its debt level." My problem with this proposal is that it assumes local governments invested badly for reasons that can be corrected. It further assumes that future behavior can be changed, perhaps by requiring local governments to promise to reform: "Eligible local governments could apply to the central government and sign a commitment promising to regulate future borrowing and refrain from excessive debt issuance, in exchange for replacing their existing high-interest debt." But that is not what happened, nor is it what will happen. Beijing has set excessively high GDP growth targets for decades. Because it could not get consumption growth to accelerate sufficiently, this meant that it also could not allow investment growth to decelerate. With the highest investment rates in history, achieving these growth targets required an enormous pipeline of economically viable investment projects. The problem is that, unlike the period before the end of the 2000s, China is no longer sufficiently underinvested across the board for huge increases in investment to generate enough viable projects and sustain real growth. China has had excess capacity in property, infrastructure, and manufacturing for many years. That necessarily means the returns on new investment have been very low or even negative. And because investment remained so high, debt had to surge – which, of course, it did. Not only has this problem not improved in recent years, it has become progressively worse. That is why local-government debt has risen so much faster since the early 2010s than the real value of provincial economies. That is why so many provinces are struggling under their debt burdens. It is not that local governments were careless or irresponsible when they made these investments. Rather, the GDP growth targets themselves forced them to continue investing, whether or not additional investment was economically justified. The inevitable result was an ever-growing volume of non-productive investment. Because provinces are now burdened by these debts, Li proposes cleaning up their balance sheets by transferring the debt onto the central government balance sheet. He is right that the central government still has a relatively clean balance sheet. It is, in fact, the last clean balance sheet left in China. But that is precisely why I think it should not be compromised in an effort to sustain GDP growth targets that have become increasingly unrealistic for an economy facing the kinds of structural problems Li himself describes. As long as Beijing continues to set excessively high GDP growth targets, there is no way to prevent debt from continuing to surge.

In fact it works in a very different way. Chinese banks mostly borrow from households to lend to businesses and government. Households are overwhelmingly net creditors to the banks while businesses and government net borrowers. Lowering interest rates, in other words, rather mechanically reduces the household income share of GDP and expand the business and government shares, which means it tends to expand production faster than consumption. We saw this most obviously in the 2000s, when Beijing engineered highly negative real interest rates to help clean up bad debt, and saw a near-collapse of the household share of GDP (and with it, the consumption share). That's why, I argued, credit expansion and lower interest rates in China actually reduce consumption and are likely to be deflationary. This, by the way, was a similar story in Japan in the 1980s and 1990s. Jinfan Zhang and Wei Xiong have just published an NBER paper that makes a very similar point. They write: "This paper investigates why China’s recurrent credit expansions have coincided with persistently weak inflation. We argue that this pattern reflects the country’s production-oriented monetary regime." "Credit expansion in China," they continue, "tends to sustain production and balance sheets rather than stimulate final demand. As a result, monetary policy operates less as a conventional tool for demand management and durable reflation, and more as a mechanism for preserving production capacity and supporting growth." That's because the income transfers needed to rebalance an economy towards consumption must come from a production side that had previously benefitted from the reverse transfers.
